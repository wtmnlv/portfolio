Ultra-processed foods (UPFs) currently make up over half of the calories in the American diet.

The term refers to food products made up of industrial ingredients and additives you wouldn’t typically find in a kitchen.

UPFs are highly marketed and designed to be low-cost, convenient, and appealing. Not so bad, right?

Well...

These foods are made of cheap, synthetic ingredients, and they’re pumped with flavorings to keep us buying.

Think of them as

edible products.

The NOVA classification system defines UPFs by sorting foods into four categories:

Unprocessed or minimally processed foods

Foods obtained directly from plants or animals with either no alterations or with minimal processes like cleaning, grinding, or fermentation.

    • Vegetables
    • Fruits
    • Legumes
    • Eggs
    • Unsalted nuts
    • Meats
    • Seafood
    • Grains

Processed ingredients

Ingredients extracted from nature or natural foods by processes such as pressing, grinding, crushing, pulverizing, and refining.

    • Salt
    • Sugar
    • Honey
    • Butter
    • Plant oils
    • Maple syrup
    • Animal fats
    • Starches

Processed foods

Foods manufactured using processed ingredients and unprocessed or minimally processed foods.

    • Artisanal bread
    • Homemade meals
    • Cheese
    • Canned vegetables
    • Homemade desserts
    • Canned fruit
    • Wine & beer
    • Canned meat & fish

Ultra-processed foods

Food products derived from foods or parts of foods with added cosmetic and industrial food additives.

    • Ready-made meals
    • Sweetened cereal
    • Supermarket bread
    • Instant soups
    • Processed meats
    • Soft drinks
    • Ice cream
    • Chocolate & candy

Recent studies indicate that most ultra-processed food has little to no health benefits.

These studies also suggest that the consumption of UPFs are linked to several negative health effects.

One study has directly linked ultra-processed foods to

32 health issues.

Heart disease, obesity, cancer, diabetes, high blood pressure, and early death are among them.

Due to their convenience and availability, ultra-processed foods are virtually unavoidable.

Here are some tips that can help you cut down on UPFs.

Cook more often.

Cooking a couple more meals a week can help lower your UPF consumption.

Look at
ingredient lists.

They are typically long and include things like stabilizers, emulsifiers, and flavorings.

Think twice
about marketing.

They’re trying to persuade you to eat their product. Healthy foods don’t need to convince you to eat them.

All that being said, don’t be too hard on yourself; it’s okay to have UPFs every so often.

Just remember to think about what you are really eating.
